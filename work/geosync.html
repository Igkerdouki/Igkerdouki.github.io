02 — GeoSync In progress

The data existed. The warning did not.

California logs every pesticide application in the state. That record is public, and it is effectively unreadable — buried in government databases nobody outside a research office can query. GeoSync turns it into a live map, a drift forecast, and an alert that reaches a school before the spray does.

The problem

Millions of pounds of chemicals, drifting without notice

Over 200 million pounds of pesticides are applied in California every year. A meaningful fraction does not stay on the field it was sprayed on — it drifts, and it drifts into places where people live: farmworker housing, playgrounds, school campuses in the Central Valley.

The people most exposed are the least informed. A parent cannot find out what was sprayed a quarter mile from their house last week. A school nurse seeing elevated illness rates has no way to check whether anything was applied nearby. The Pesticide Use Report data that would answer both questions is published — and unusable.

The information exists. The protection does not.

That gap is the entire project. GeoSync is not a data collection effort. It is a translation layer over a dataset the state already maintains.

Grounding the work

Twenty users, one shared question

We wrote twenty user stories before writing any code, spanning the range of people who would touch this: parents, beekeepers, farmers, school administrators, public health researchers, farm inspectors, farmworkers.

The stories diverged wildly in framing but converged on one underlying need — what was sprayed near this location, and should I change my behavior because of it? A pregnant mother, a daycare provider, and a grocery buyer are all asking that question with different stakes attached. Recognizing that let us build one query engine underneath and vary only the presentation.

The parent

"I want my child's cumulative exposure score so I can discuss cancer risk with their doctor."

The school administrator

"I want automatic alerts when drift is approaching campus so I can keep children indoors."

The farmer

"I want AI-optimized dosage recommendations so I use only what's needed and save money."

The organic grower

"I want proof when a neighboring farm's drift contaminates my crops so I can protect my certification."

The farmer stories mattered more than they first appeared. A tool that only accuses growers gets adopted by nobody who actually holds a sprayer. Dosage optimization gave the same platform a reason to be useful to the people generating the data — which is the only version of this that survives contact with the real world.

Modeling

Physics first, learning second

Drift prediction is not a pure machine learning problem. Atmospheric dispersion has well-established physics, and ignoring it in favor of fitting a model to sparse labels would have been both less accurate and less defensible. I paired a Gaussian plume model with learned components where the data could actually support them.

Drift predictor
Gaussian plume dispersion combined with gradient boosting — physics sets the shape of the plume, the learned layer corrects it against observed conditions.
Atmospheric stability
Stability class derived from live weather, since the same application disperses very differently on a calm night than a windy afternoon.
Dosage optimizer
Planned next: an XGBoost model trained on California PUR records, recommending application amounts from crop, pest, and field size.
Anomaly detector
Isolation Forest flagging unusual spray patterns — including possible unreported events.
Risk scorer
Multi-factor regression producing an exposure score for any address, combining proximity, chemical toxicity, and frequency.
NLP query engine
Retrieval over the spray database so a question in plain English — "what was sprayed near my house last month?" — returns a grounded answer.

The weather dependency is what makes this an early-warning system rather than a historical archive. Drift direction is a function of conditions at the moment of application, so the same spray event produces a completely different risk footprint depending on when it happens.

The platform

From a database row to a decision

Live map dashboard

Spray events plotted with color-coded risk zones, overlaid with school and farm locations so proximity is immediately visible.

Drift forecast

Spray location plus current weather returns a heat map of where the chemical is actually heading.

Exposure scoring

Enter an address, get a weekly exposure score and full historical lookup for that location.

Toxicity database

EPA classifications, carcinogenicity, and health concerns attached to each chemical — so a name in a record becomes meaningful.

Alerts

SMS and email triggered when predicted drift approaches a registered school or home.

Research export

Bulk CSV and JSON export up to 100K rows, with trend and comparison endpoints for public health researchers.

The research tooling turned out to matter as much as the consumer-facing map. A public health researcher correlating exposure with cancer incidence by census tract needs bulk access and reproducible queries, not a dashboard — so the API exposes county analysis, chemical comparison, and monthly and yearly time series directly.

Outcome

A public dataset made usable

GeoSync ships as a working platform — a FastAPI backend serving spray queries, drift prediction, toxicity lookups, and research exports, with a React and MapLibre front end over PostGIS.

What I would keep building is validation. The plume model is physically grounded, but confirming predicted drift against measured deposition in the field is the step that would take this from a credible forecast to a defensible one — and it is the step that would let a school actually act on an alert without hesitation.

The reason I picked this project is not complicated. Bee colony losses hit their highest recorded level last year, and childhood cancer rates in agricultural regions have been climbing for decades. Those are current facts, not projections. Building a warning layer over data the state already publishes is one of the few interventions available that requires no new legislation and no new sensors.
