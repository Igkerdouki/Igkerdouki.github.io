WildSafe Deployed

It spots the deer before the driver does.

WildSafe watches roadside cameras for animals and warns drivers before a collision. I built the machine learning on CLIP, OpenAI's pre-trained vision-language model, tuned to our species through prompt engineering — and the accuracy was fantastic: 94–99% per species.

The problem

A deer is visible long before it is an emergency

The project started with a teammate's collision. Looking into it, the scale turned out to be much larger than one bad night on a back road.

1.9M
Wildlife-vehicle collisions per year in the US
200
Deaths annually
$8B
In damages each year

The existing fixes — wildlife overpasses, fenced corridors, embedded roadway sensors — work, and cost millions per mile. That price is why most dangerous stretches of road have nothing at all. We wanted to know whether a camera and a good model could cover a corridor for the price of a Raspberry Pi.

The animal is always in frame before it's in the road. Something just has to be watching.

The model

Three approaches, and only one worked

Wildlife detection looks like a solved object-detection problem until you try it. Animals appear at night, at distance, partly hidden by brush, across twelve species that share silhouettes. I worked through three approaches before one held up.

YOLO-World, zero-shot

Open-vocabulary detection straight out of the box. Fast to try, and too imprecise on the species distinctions that matter.

YOLO, fine-tuned

The obvious next move: train on our own labeled clips. Our dataset was too small to teach a detector every species across the lighting a real roadside sees.

CLIP, zero-shot — 94–99% per species

Instead of training a classifier, describe the classes in language and let a vision-language model match images against those descriptions. No training run and no labeling bottleneck — and the species list becomes editable text rather than a retraining job. Prompt wording turned out to matter as much as any hyperparameter.

The lesson I keep from this: with a small dataset, a large pre-trained model used well beats a smaller model trained on whatever you can afford to label. We got further by not training at all.

Shipping it

Fitting the model into 300 MB

A working model on a laptop is not a deployed system. Our inference stack started at roughly 1.5 GB — well past what the cloud instance could hold, which meant the service would come up looking healthy and then die under real load.

Converting to ONNX Runtime brought it to about 300 MB, an 80% reduction, and that is what separated a demo from something that stays up.

Capture
A Raspberry Pi camera unit at the roadside, streaming continuously.
Transport
WebRTC to the hosted inference service. The peer-to-peer connection was the least stable part of the system and took real NAT traversal debugging.
Inference
CLIP zero-shot classification on ONNX Runtime, hosted on Render.
Alert
A Flutter mobile app with map-based location, delivered in under a second end to end.
Deterrent
Species-specific tones played at the roadside — a 15 kHz pulse for deer, an 800 Hz sweep for bears — to move the animal before the car arrives.

The deterrent is the part I like most as product thinking. Warning the driver only solves half of it; the other half is getting the animal off the road. Different species hear different ranges, so a single tone would have been useless — the detection has to be species-aware for the response to be.

Outcome

We did a great job on this one. The model is accurate, the whole chain runs from camera to phone in under a second, and the project won IEEE Quarterly Projects in Spring 2026.

The same camera also watches for people — telling ordinary activity apart from someone fallen or in distress. That is the pose-estimation problem I went on to build properly in Perceptra.

Where it would go next

Solar-powered roadside units so deployment does not depend on mains power; edge inference directly on a Raspberry Pi 5 to take the network off the critical path; and pilots with a state DOT on corridors that already have collision histories. The technical work is further along than the deployment story — a model that works is not yet a system anyone has agreed to install.
